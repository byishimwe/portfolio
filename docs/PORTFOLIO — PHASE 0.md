# **00\. The Project in One Sentence**

Build a visually distinctive, professionally credible, fully realized personal portfolio that demonstrates exceptional design taste and frontend-development ability, presents three strong projects through a signature editorial gallery, tells their stories through complete case studies, and converts interested visitors into potential web-design and development clients.

The portfolio is not simply a collection of past work.

**It is simultaneously a sales asset, a design statement, an interactive exhibition, and a demonstration of execution quality.**

It needs to work for two audiences:

1. A business owner evaluating whether to hire us.  
2. A designer, developer, or creative professional evaluating our craft.

The first audience determines our commercial priorities.

The second raises the standard of visual and technical execution.

Both should experience the same coherent, high-quality website.

## **00.1 The central product idea**

The portfolio is organized around a signature experience called **The Living Frame**.

Instead of presenting three conventional project cards, the selected work appears through one recognizable editorial gallery.

As visitors move between projects:

* The displayed imagery changes.  
* The surrounding atmosphere evolves.  
* The composition retains its underlying identity.  
* The visual language adapts to each project.  
* Project information remains accessible.  
* Visitors can open complete individual case studies.  
* The selected project image becomes the visual connection between the homepage and its case-study page.

The experience should feel like moving through a carefully curated exhibition and then entering the world of a particular project.

## **00.2 The complete product**

The intended product includes:

**A. An editorial homepage**

The entry point for understanding who we are, what we do, and the work we create.

**B. The Living Frame**

The signature project gallery and primary interactive experience.

**C. Three individual case studies**

Dedicated pages providing context, design rationale, genuine imagery, functional details, and implementation insights.

**D. Spatially continuous navigation**

An enhanced transition connecting the active homepage project with its corresponding case-study hero.

**E. A commercial conversion experience**

Clearly presented services, concise personal positioning, and direct contact through WhatsApp or email.

**F. A production-quality implementation**

Responsive, accessible, performant, reliable, and ready to share publicly.

## **00.3 The launch commitment**

**The complete experience is the target for today's build.**

We are not asking Codex to construct an intentionally reduced homepage prototype and postpone the defining parts of the portfolio.

The three case studies, Living Frame, and supported-browser shared-element transitions are included in the intended implementation.

However, ambitious functionality does not justify broken navigation, inaccessible content, unstable scrolling, or a failed deployment.

A technical fallback is allowed when a browser or device cannot support an enhancement.

That is different from omitting the feature entirely.

7. Secondary decorative refinement

Success is not:

* Building a generic developer portfolio under a different name.  
* Spending so much time polishing that no usable public website is available for outreach.

# **06\. The Signature Concept — The Living Frame**

## **6.1 Definition**

**The Living Frame is an editorial gallery where the work changes the surrounding atmosphere while the visual language remains continuous.**

The visitor moves between projects through one recognizable system of:

* Framing  
* Composition  
* Typography  
* Imagery  
* Movement  
* Depth  
* Project information  
* Navigation

The frame acts as the portfolio's visual identity.

The projects supply the changing character.

## **6.2 Creative balance**

**70% Editorial Exhibition / 30% Cinematic Camera**

### **Editorial characteristics**

* Precise grids  
* Disciplined spacing  
* Refined typography  
* Strong image framing  
* Intentional alignment  
* Quiet hierarchy  
* Editorial numbering  
* Consistent geometry

### **Cinematic characteristics**

* Layered depth  
* Masked reveals  
* Gentle image movement  
* Atmospheric shifts  
* Spatial continuity  
* Deliberate pacing  
* Controlled transformations

## **6.3 Emotional impression**

The visitor should feel like they are moving through a carefully curated exhibition of digital work.

Each project introduces its own atmosphere without destroying the portfolio's overall visual identity.

## **6.4 Desktop gallery model**

The desktop gallery uses:

**A persistent visual stage**

A large image frame that remains recognizable as the selected project changes.

**A naturally scrolling editorial information rail**

Project descriptions and actions arranged as semantic chapters.

**A project index**

Direct navigation between the three projects.

### **Intended interaction**

As a visitor scrolls through the project chapters:

1. The active project changes.  
2. The stage prepares the incoming imagery.  
3. The image transforms within the existing frame.  
4. The stage atmosphere changes.  
5. The project index updates.  
6. The information remains accessible.  
7. The visitor continues scrolling naturally.

The transition must work in both directions.

## **6.5 Gallery layout principle**

The visual frame dominates the composition.

The information rail supports it.

The desktop gallery begins with approximately:

* 67% visual stage  
* Appropriate editorial gap  
* Remaining width for information

Exact responsive dimensions belong to Phase 2\.

## **6.6 Persistent geometry**

The underlying outer frame geometry remains stable between projects.

Do not transform the gallery into three unrelated cards or entirely different layouts.

Project-specific variation happens primarily through:

* Image content  
* Internal framing  
* Atmosphere  
* Controlled motion  
* Metadata emphasis

## **6.7 Project atmospheres**

### **Café Bliss**

Warm, inviting, tactile, editorial.

### **IMIZI**

Strong, grounded, architectural.

### **Quad**

Precise, structured, digital.

The overall portfolio identity remains consistent.

## **6.8 Natural scrolling**

The gallery must preserve native document scrolling.

Do not:

* Hijack wheel input  
* Trap visitors in a pinned timeline  
* Require fixed animation completion before scrolling  
* Force mandatory snapping  
* Require unusual gestures  
* Hide projects behind a proprietary navigation system

The animation responds to the visitor.

It does not control them.

## **6.9 Project discoverability**

Visitors must be able to identify and reach all three projects directly.

The project index should allow navigation to the relevant chapter.

Project details and links must remain meaningful without animation.

## **6.10 Mobile gallery**

Mobile and tablet layouts use a deliberately designed stacked presentation.

Each project receives:

* Its number and category  
* Strong imagery  
* Clear title  
* Description  
* Project details  
* Live link  
* Case-study link

The mobile version is not a broken or shrunken desktop gallery.

Its typography, spacing, imagery, and transitions must be designed intentionally.

## **6.11 Full signature experience**

The Living Frame has two connected parts.

### **Part A — Gallery transformation**

The active project changes within the recognizable visual stage.

This includes:

* Image transitions  
* Atmospheric changes  
* Project synchronization  
* Controlled depth  
* Editorial framing  
* Reverse interaction  
* Responsive adaptation

### **Part B — Entering the project**

When a visitor selects **Explore Project**, the visual experience continues into the dedicated case-study page.

The project image should feel like the same visual object moving into a new composition.

These parts form one signature concept.

They should not feel like two unrelated animation experiments.

## **6.12 Shared-element route transition**

Intended sequence:

1. A project is visible in the Living Frame.  
2. The visitor activates Explore Project.  
3. The selected image becomes the visual anchor.  
4. The surrounding homepage composition recedes.  
5. The image expands or repositions toward the case-study hero.  
6. The case-study layout appears.  
7. The image settles into its new composition.  
8. The visitor continues reading the page normally.

The transition should preserve visual continuity.

It must not merely replace the whole page with an arbitrary fade to black.

## **6.13 Browser compatibility**

Use the supported route-transition mechanism where available.

For environments without support:

* Navigate normally.  
* Preserve the correct page.  
* Preserve functional browser history.  
* Use a simpler transition or immediate route change.  
* Do not block access to the case study.

The lack of advanced browser support is not an excuse to disable navigation.

## **6.14 Direct navigation**

Opening a case study directly must work without an originating homepage frame.

The page must display correctly.

No transition is required when no source element exists.

## **6.15 Reverse navigation**

Browser back and internal Back to Work links must behave correctly.

Where reliable, the return transition may restore the feeling of reentering the gallery.

Where the required geometry cannot be restored safely, prioritize proper history and scroll restoration with a simpler visual transition.

## **6.16 Essential behavioral rules**

1. Work remains understandable without motion.  
2. The gallery never prevents access to links.  
3. Available projects are discoverable quickly.  
4. Motion does not block ordinary scrolling.  
5. Desktop and mobile may have different choreography.  
6. Reduced-motion preferences are respected.  
7. Project imagery remains the visual focus.  
8. Other animations remain quieter than the gallery.  
9. Direct case-study navigation works.  
10. Browser history remains correct.  
11. Rapid scrolling cannot permanently desynchronize the stage.  
12. Reverse scrolling must work.  
13. Resizing must not leave broken sticky or animation states.  
14. Animation failures must not hide essential content.  
15. Route transitions must not conflict with gallery animation.  
16. The stage must never become a persistent empty frame.  
17. Image loading must not cause uncontrolled layout shifts.

# **14\. Risks and Mitigations**

## **Risk 1 — Overdesigning**

**Problem:** Too much time is spent exploring visual variants.

**Mitigation:** Use the established Phase 2 design system. Refine execution rather than repeatedly reopening art direction.

## **Risk 2 — The signature interaction introduces instability**

**Problem:** Gallery transitions or shared-element navigation produce broken visual states, scroll bugs, or route conflicts.

**Mitigation:** Keep animation ownership separate. Build functionality first, then apply the full interaction. Verify real browser behavior.

## **Risk 3 — Weak project imagery**

**Problem:** The projects look underwhelming because screenshots are poorly chosen or cropped.

**Mitigation:** Prioritize authentic, high-quality imagery and inspect its composition in the actual gallery.

## **Risk 4 — Missing Quad authenticated screenshots**

**Problem:** The publicly visible login page does not adequately demonstrate the product.

**Mitigation:** Inspect existing approved repository assets or acquire safe genuine application screenshots. Do not fabricate internal screens.

## **Risk 5 — Generic case studies**

**Problem:** Three pages exist, but they contain generic headings and repeated marketing language.

**Mitigation:** Require project-specific design, interaction, and technical details supported by the actual projects.

## **Risk 6 — Unclear commercial offer**

**Problem:** Visitors admire the design but cannot tell what services are offered.

**Mitigation:** Keep the hero explanation and services section clear and business-oriented.

## **Risk 7 — Fictional projects appear misleading**

**Problem:** Concept work is described as real client delivery.

**Mitigation:** Use visible, tactful concept-project identification and accurate descriptions.

## **Risk 8 — Broken contact path**

**Problem:** A prospect wants to reach us but encounters incorrect or missing contact information.

**Mitigation:** Verify WhatsApp and email destinations before public launch.

## **Risk 9 — Mobile compromises**

**Problem:** Desktop interactions become awkward, slow, or unreadable on phones.

**Mitigation:** Use the deliberately designed mobile gallery and test actual mobile viewport behavior.

## **Risk 10 — Route-transition complexity**

**Problem:** Shared-element animations conflict with browser navigation or GSAP.

**Mitigation:** Use clear animation responsibilities, stable source/destination identities, and a functional navigation fallback.

## **Risk 11 — Performance degradation**

**Problem:** Large images and animation layers make the site slow.

**Mitigation:** Optimize imagery, reserve space, reduce unnecessary animation work, and test actual behavior.

## **Risk 12 — Incomplete validation**

**Problem:** Generated code appears finished but contains hidden defects.

**Mitigation:** Run automated checks and inspect real rendered pages. Document untested behavior honestly.

## **Risk 13 — Launch delay**

**Problem:** Excessive polishing prevents outreach from starting.

**Mitigation:** Work through implementation checkpoints efficiently and keep the commercial deadline visible. Avoid unrelated features and endless visual experiments.

## **Risk 14 — Contradictory specifications**

**Problem:** Codex receives older documents treating routes and transitions as optional.

**Mitigation:** Use revised Phase 0, Phase 1, and Phase 2 alongside Phase 3, with consistent definitions of the required full build.
